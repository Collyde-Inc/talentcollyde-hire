### Observation 1 — "I'll talk myself into another Will"

You named this twice in the recording (Transcript [10:37] and [12:26]) — once as a pattern, once as an explicit ask. Charm and aesthetic fluency keep getting you to "yes" before operational competence has been tested. You said this once (Transcript [08:30]) — but it's one of the sharpest pieces of self-diagnosis in the recording. Network hires in the wine community have high aesthetic agreement, which reads as cultural fit on first impression but collapses under pressure. The decoupling probe: how does a candidate behave when they don't have shared context.

*In the rubric:* Operational Judgment scored separately from Cultural Resonance, so a strong vibe can't compensate for an empty judgment score. *In the kit:* a "decision under unfamiliar conditions" block — a forced scenario outside wine where the candidate has to reason from first principles, not from shared community vocabulary.

## 1. The company

Sutter & Vine is a natural wine subscription. ~3,200 active subscribers at $120/month get four curated bottles, focused on small biodynamic and low-intervention producers. Customer profile: late thirties to early fifties, household income north of $150K, urban, food-aware. Profitable for two years, ~$4.5M ARR, growing ~20% YoY. Bootstrapped — no patient capital. Every hire is real money.

> *"We're not VC-backed. Bootstrapped. Which matters because every hire is real money out of my pocket — no patient capital here."* — Transcript [01:13]

---

## 2. Mission and values

**Mission:** Route around the twenty huge distributors who decide what 99% of Americans drink. Be the path that lets a small biodynamic vineyard in the Loire sell direct to a household in Portland with both sides getting a fair deal.

> *"Small producers — people doing real, interesting work — they can't get to a US market without going through one of those gatekeepers, who take half the margin and don't care if the bottle is great or just sellable."* — Transcript [01:47]

**Values:**

### Care about the producer
> *"If someone on our team can't tell me the name of the winemaker behind a label we sold last month, that's bad."* — Transcript [02:38]

**Moment it was real:** A Loire producer (Pierre) had a bad 2024 harvest and was going to lose his lease. Sutter & Vine bought his whole inventory at full price — including bottles that were oxidized — and took the loss. He sent Maya a Christmas card. *"That's what that value means."* — Transcript [03:48]

### Keep our word
> *"Subscriber tells us they don't like reds, we don't send them reds."* — Transcript [02:38]

**Moment:** A Brooklyn customer emailed 11 times asking why she kept getting bold reds. The CS hire who's no longer there would issue $5 credits. Brittany — the one who lasted — read the chain, called the customer, listened, and rebuilt her profile. The customer has been a subscriber for four years.

### Make less, but make it right
> *"We don't ship discount mystery boxes. We don't do a Black Friday push. Our margins are tighter than they could be because we don't cut corners on sourcing."* — Transcript [03:00]

**Moment:** Passed on a producer offering 30% margin (vs. their usual 12%) because the wine was mediocre. Left ~$80K on the table that year.

### Say the hard thing
> *"If someone has a bad pour or a producer is slipping, we name it. We don't paper over."* — Transcript [03:15]

**Moment:** A producer started cutting his blends with bulk wine without disclosure. Maya called him out, he gaslit her, Sutter & Vine cut him. He told another importer they were "difficult." *"Fine."* — Transcript [05:25]

## 3. What thrives here

People-people who happen to work at a wine company — not "wine people." The archetype is Brittany: came in with no wine background (formerly an ICU nurse), showed up with a notebook her first week, wrote down everything anyone said. Three months in, she knew more about Sutter & Vine's subscribers than Maya does.

> *"She's not a wine person. She's a person person who happens to work at a wine company. That's the thing."* — Transcript [05:38]

The trait under the trait: a willingness to be a novice in public. People who think they need to perform expertise out of the gate don't last here. People who can listen and document and let the patterns surface do.

---

## 4. What dies here

People who substitute domain fluency for operational follow-through. The archetype is Will — a certified sommelier from a Michelin restaurant. Beautiful palate. Could not run inventory. Would write 600 words about a wine and never get the producer their PO. Eight months before he was let go.

> *"The hard part is — I knew. By month two I knew. I kept hoping it would click. He was so passionate about the wine."* — Transcript [06:21]

The pattern beneath the pattern: passion for the topic substituting for evidence of execution. Anyone whose stories are about *what they know* rather than *what they got done* is at high risk of failing here. This is the highest-signal section in this file — and the most relevant input for the Operations hire currently in intake.

## 5. Hiring philosophy

**Speed vs. fit:** Stated answer is fit. Real answer is fit *unless* under peak-season pressure, in which case Maya defaults to hiring the most enthusiastic person in the room. The Will hire happened during a scramble.

> *"When I get behind on something, I move fast and I'm worse at fit."* — Transcript [07:25]

**Sourcing posture:** Network-first in practice, despite saying she'll cast wider. Last three hires came through the wine community. Two worked out, one (Will) didn't. Maya named the underlying mechanism out loud — see Observation 2.

> *"I keep saying I'll cast wide. I keep hiring from my network."* — Transcript [08:07]

**Decision-making:** Maya owns the call for most hires. For senior roles, the CFO (Iris) runs a final round — she's blunt and catches what Maya misses. **For the Operations role specifically, Maya wants Iris involved earlier, not later** — that's a direct intake signal worth respecting.

## 6. Founder profile

**How Maya decides:** Notices the *quality of questions* a candidate asks back. Brittany asked four specific questions about how Maya would handle a customer complaint — checking whether she could do the job the way she wanted to, not testing the founder. Iris told Maya she'd quit if Maya tried to add a VC round without her buy-in — clarity about her own non-negotiables. **The tell: people who know what they will and won't do.**

**What Maya's ignored:** Two patterns, both predictive, both still occasionally talked past when tired:
1. Candidate only talks about the domain, never about the work of running a business.
2. Candidate cannot name what they don't want to do or aren't good at.

Both showed up in the Will hire. Both are explicit red flags Maya wants the next hire to be screened against (see Observation 1).

**Candidate experience:** Every candidate should leave feeling they learned something — even the ones who get a "no." Maya writes rejection notes by hand. Some have become subscribers.

> *"I won't ghost. I write rejection notes by hand. Some of those have become customers."* — Transcript [11:42]
